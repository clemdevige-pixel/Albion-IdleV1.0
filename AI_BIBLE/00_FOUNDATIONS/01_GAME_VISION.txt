# 1. PURPOSE OF THIS DOCUMENT

This document defines the intended player experience of Albion Idle.

It translates the rules of the Project Bible into a clear creative direction.

It explains:

* what kind of game is being created;
* what fantasy the player experiences;
* what the player repeatedly does;
* why the player progresses;
* why the player returns;
* what makes the game strategically interesting;
* what the game must never become.

This document does not define:

* final balancing values;
* exact combat formulas;
* final mastery trees;
* final zone content;
* final resource lists;
* final monster lists;
* technical architecture;
* detailed implementation rules.

Those subjects belong in dedicated system, data and technical documents.

If this document contradicts the Project Bible, the Project Bible takes priority.

---

# 2. HIGH-CONCEPT STATEMENT

Albion Idle is a single-player idle RPG in which one persistent hero gathers resources, crafts equipment, develops specialized masteries and prepares different builds to conquer increasingly dangerous regions.

The game combines:

* long-term idle progression;
* character-driven RPG progression;
* equipment crafting;
* automatic combat;
* strategic preparation;
* build optimization;
* increasingly demanding power checks.

The game continues progressing while the player is absent, but the player’s decisions determine how effectively that time is used.

---

# 3. ONE-SENTENCE PLAYER PROMISE

> Build your hero through what you practice, forge the equipment you need and adapt your strategy to conquer every region.

---

# 4. CORE PLAYER FANTASY

The player begins as an inexperienced adventurer with limited equipment, limited knowledge and access only to relatively safe territory.

Through repeated action, the player becomes:

* a more experienced fighter;
* a more efficient gatherer;
* a more capable crafter;
* a better-prepared explorer;
* a more knowledgeable strategist.

The fantasy is not merely becoming numerically stronger.

The fantasy is becoming increasingly competent in the world.

The player should feel:

> I can now overcome this challenge because I prepared better, understood the problem and improved the right parts of my character.

---

# 5. PLAYER ROLE

The player controls one persistent hero.

The player is simultaneously:

* an adventurer;
* a fighter;
* a gatherer;
* a crafter;
* an optimizer;
* a planner.

The player is not:

* a distant ruler controlling disposable units;
* the manager of a hero collection;
* the owner of a multiplayer economic empire;
* a passive observer with no strategic responsibility.

The character performs many actions automatically.

The player decides:

* what the character should do;
* where the character should go;
* what equipment should be used;
* which masteries should be developed;
* which resources are currently important;
* which challenge should be attempted;
* when preparation is sufficient;
* when a strategy should change.

---

# 6. TARGET EXPERIENCE

The game should create a recurring mixture of five feelings.

## 6.1 Progress

The player should regularly see meaningful improvement.

Examples include:

* gaining mastery experience;
* collecting a needed resource;
* completing an equipment piece;
* improving combat efficiency;
* unlocking a recipe;
* unlocking an ability;
* reaching a new stage;
* defeating a previously impossible enemy.

Progress should remain visible even when access to the next major region is still far away.

---

## 6.2 Anticipation

The player should regularly know what they are working toward.

Examples:

* the next equipment tier;
* the final material needed for a weapon;
* a mastery unlock;
* a new active ability;
* a difficult mini-boss;
* the zone boss;
* the next danger zone.

Long-term objectives should be supported by short-term milestones.

---

## 6.3 Preparation

The player should feel responsible for the result of important encounters.

Before a difficult challenge, the player may need to:

* improve equipment;
* complete a build;
* change weapon;
* change active abilities;
* select another passive;
* craft food or potions;
* improve a relevant mastery;
* repair or replace damaged gear;
* choose a more efficient farming location.

Preparation should be visible in the outcome.

---

## 6.4 Discovery

New stages and regions should introduce more than higher numbers.

The player should discover:

* new resources;
* new enemies;
* new encounter patterns;
* new recipes;
* new strategic problems;
* new environmental identities;
* new progression possibilities.

The exact content will be defined later.

The design requirement is that progression must regularly reveal something new.

---

## 6.5 Mastery

The player should gradually understand how the game’s systems interact.

Early progression may be solved through obvious equipment upgrades.

Later progression should increasingly reward:

* build understanding;
* resource planning;
* weapon knowledge;
* ability selection;
* risk management;
* efficient use of offline time;
* adaptation to enemy mechanics.

The player should feel more knowledgeable, not only more powerful.

---

# 7. EXPERIENCE PILLARS

## 7.1 One hero, many possible identities

The player does not select a permanent class.

The character’s identity emerges from what the player uses and practices.

A character may become experienced with:

* swords;
* bows;
* axes;
* defensive equipment;
* offensive equipment;
* mining;
* skinning;
* refining;
* crafting.

Changing direction should remain possible.

Specialization should provide value without permanently imprisoning the player in an early choice.

---

## 7.2 Adventure supported by production

Gathering, refining and crafting are not detached side systems.

They directly prepare the player for adventure.

The intended relationship is:

```text
Adventure reveals a need
↓
The player identifies the required improvement
↓
Production creates that improvement
↓
The hero returns stronger or better prepared
↓
The challenge is overcome
```

Example:

```text
A boss defeats the player
↓
The player identifies insufficient survivability
↓
The player gathers and refines defensive materials
↓
The player crafts or improves armor
↓
The player changes food or abilities
↓
The player defeats the boss
```

Production must have a visible purpose.

---

## 7.3 Automatic execution, meaningful direction

The hero can automatically:

* attack;
* repeat combat;
* gather;
* refine;
* craft;
* continue a configured activity.

Automation executes the player’s plan.

Automation must not replace the player’s plan.

The interesting decision occurs before or between activities:

* selecting an objective;
* configuring a build;
* choosing a location;
* deciding what to produce;
* setting acceptable risk;
* changing strategy after failure.

---

## 7.4 Slow conquest, frequent satisfaction

Conquering a complete zone should require meaningful investment.

The player should not complete a major chapter immediately.

However, long-term progression must contain frequent rewards.

The player should not need to wait several hours before seeing any result.

The game should alternate between:

* small gains;
* useful unlocks;
* equipment improvements;
* new challenges;
* major victories.

Macro progression is slow.

Feedback and rewards are frequent.

---

## 7.5 Understandable depth

The game should be deep without being unnecessarily obscure.

The player should be able to understand:

* what their equipment does;
* why their damage changed;
* why they survived or died;
* what a mastery unlocked;
* what a consumable contributed;
* why a farming location is efficient or inefficient.

Depth should emerge from combinations and decisions rather than from a very large number of unexplained statistics.

---

# 8. CORE EXPERIENCE LOOP

The central player experience is:

```text
Discover a new objective
↓
Attempt the content
↓
Observe performance or failure
↓
Identify what should improve
↓
Gather the required resources
↓
Refine and craft
↓
Improve equipment and masteries
↓
Configure the character
↓
Attempt the content again
↓
Overcome the challenge
↓
Unlock a new objective
```

This loop can occur on several scales.

## Short scale

* defeat enemies;
* collect loot;
* gain resources;
* improve mastery;
* complete a production task.

## Session scale

* craft an item;
* improve a build;
* unlock a stage;
* defeat a mini-boss;
* solve an efficiency problem.

## Chapter scale

* optimize a tier;
* complete a zone;
* defeat the final boss;
* enter the next danger level.

---

# 9. THE ROLE OF FAILURE

Failure is not only a punishment.

Failure is information.

A failed attempt should help the player answer questions such as:

* Did I lack damage?
* Did I lack survivability?
* Was my weapon unsuitable?
* Did I use the wrong ability?
* Did I need a consumable?
* Was my mastery too low?
* Did I attempt the content too early?
* Was the risk greater than the expected reward?

The game should make the cause of failure understandable enough to support a new plan.

Failure must not feel like an unexplained numerical rejection.

---

# 10. PROGRESSION PHILOSOPHY

## 10.1 Progress through practice

The character becomes experienced in what the character does.

The player does not receive a universal pool of experience that completely ignores the activity performed.

A player who repeatedly uses a weapon should become more capable with that weapon.

A player who repeatedly practices a profession should become more efficient in that profession.

---

## 10.2 Equipment as immediate power

Equipment provides a major part of the hero’s immediate capability.

Crafting or acquiring a better item should create a visible improvement.

Equipment must remain valuable throughout the game.

Permanent mastery progression must not make equipment irrelevant.

---

## 10.3 Mastery as permanent growth

Masteries represent long-term character development.

They may provide:

* efficiency;
* unlocks;
* additional choices;
* specialization;
* improved use of equipment;
* new abilities;
* access to advanced production.

The exact effects will be defined in system documents.

---

## 10.4 Zones as chapters

Each color zone represents a major chapter.

A chapter should have:

* an introduction;
* new opportunities;
* growing resistance;
* optimization requirements;
* one or more important checks;
* a final confrontation;
* a meaningful conclusion.

The player should feel that a zone was understood and conquered before leaving it.

---

# 11. POWER-CHECK PHILOSOPHY

Power checks are central to the game’s identity.

They determine whether the player is ready for the next step.

## 11.1 Soft power checks

A soft check allows the player to continue, but with poor performance.

Examples:

* slow combat;
* inefficient gathering;
* low resource yield;
* excessive repair costs;
* poor offline results.

The player is encouraged to optimize.

---

## 11.2 Hard power checks

A hard check blocks progression.

Examples:

* a mandatory boss cannot be defeated;
* the hero dies before completing an encounter;
* damage is insufficient to overcome an enemy mechanic;
* a required stage cannot be completed.

The player must improve or adapt before continuing.

---

## 11.3 Strategic checks

A strategic or knowledge check evaluates the suitability of the player’s choices.

The player may already possess enough total power but use it incorrectly.

Possible solutions include:

* changing weapon;
* changing active abilities;
* changing passive;
* changing armor;
* using a different consumable;
* changing the balance between offense and defense.

Strategic checks should not become arbitrary puzzle locks.

The player must have enough information to reason toward a solution.

---

# 12. COMBAT EXPERIENCE

Combat is automatic at its foundation.

The hero attacks and follows normal combat behavior without requiring constant input.

The player’s involvement comes through:

* equipment selection;
* active ability selection;
* passive selection;
* consumable preparation;
* strategic activation of limited actions;
* deciding when to retreat or change plans.

The current target loadout is:

* two active weapon abilities;
* one passive weapon ability.

Combat must remain readable.

The player should be able to understand:

* who is dealing damage;
* how quickly health changes;
* when an ability activates;
* why an important effect occurred;
* why an encounter was won or lost.

Combat should not become visually or numerically incomprehensible when automated.

---

# 13. IDLE EXPERIENCE

## 13.1 The game continues while the player is absent

The hero may continue a configured activity while the player is offline.

The base offline progression cap is eight hours.

Long-term progression may increase this duration.

---

## 13.2 Offline progression rewards planning

Before leaving, the player should make decisions such as:

* which activity to perform;
* which zone or stage to farm;
* which build to use;
* which resources to target;
* which consumables may be used;
* how much risk is acceptable.

The offline result reflects these decisions.

---

## 13.3 Returning to the game

When the player returns, the game should clearly communicate:

* elapsed offline duration;
* activity performed;
* resources gained;
* enemies defeated;
* mastery gained;
* consumables used;
* durability lost;
* interruptions or failures;
* new unlocks.

The return screen should transform elapsed time into understandable progression information.

The exact interface will be defined later.

---

## 13.4 Idle must not trivialize challenges

The player should not be able to bypass every hard wall simply by remaining offline for longer.

Offline progression may provide the resources needed to improve.

It must not automatically invalidate preparation, build choices or boss mechanics.

---

# 14. WORLD EXPERIENCE

The world contains five major danger families:

1. Blue;
2. Yellow;
3. Orange;
4. Red;
5. Black.

Each zone color should create an increasing sense of:

* danger;
* consequence;
* opportunity;
* preparation requirements;
* strategic complexity.

The player should understand that entering a more dangerous region is a meaningful commitment.

The final world content remains open, including:

* exact resources;
* exact monster families;
* cities;
* settlements;
* dungeons;
* events;
* special encounters;
* stage identities.

These elements must later support the chapter identity of each zone.

---

# 15. RISK EXPERIENCE

Risk should escalate gradually.

## Blue Zone

The player learns and experiments safely.

Failure should have little or no meaningful long-term consequence.

## Yellow Zone

The player begins to experience equipment wear and mild consequences.

Risk becomes visible but remains forgiving.

## Orange Zone

Preparation and repeated failure begin to carry noticeable costs.

## Red Zone

The player must seriously consider:

* equipment value;
* durability;
* efficiency;
* risk tolerance;
* retreat decisions.

## Black Zone

The player enters highly dangerous progression where repeated failure can eventually contribute to permanent equipment loss.

The exact numerical rules will be defined later.

The emotional objective is:

> Higher danger should make success more valuable, not make failure feel arbitrary.

---

# 16. REASONS TO RETURN

The player should return to the game because they want to:

* collect the result of an offline plan;
* complete an item;
* unlock an ability;
* reach a mastery threshold;
* test a new build;
* retry a boss;
* enter a new stage;
* improve a production chain;
* respond to a newly discovered progression need.

The game should avoid depending only on:

* daily login rewards;
* artificial appointment mechanics;
* fear of missing out;
* arbitrary streak systems.

Return motivation should primarily come from player-owned progression goals.

---

# 17. PLAYER DECISION TYPES

The game should regularly present decisions in five categories.

## 17.1 Objective decisions

What should I work toward next?

Examples:

* equipment;
* mastery;
* recipe;
* stage;
* boss;
* offline capacity.

## 17.2 Production decisions

What should I gather, refine or craft?

## 17.3 Build decisions

What equipment, weapon, abilities and consumables should I use?

## 17.4 Risk decisions

Where can I farm safely and efficiently?

## 17.5 Time decisions

Which activity provides the best use of my active or offline time?

A healthy experience should not reduce every decision to a single obvious efficiency calculation.

Different goals may justify different choices.

---

# 18. DESIRED PLAYER STORIES

The final game should be capable of producing player experiences such as:

> I could not defeat the boss, so I changed from an offensive setup to a more defensive build and survived long enough to win.

> I spent my offline period gathering the materials needed for a new weapon, crafted it when I returned and immediately felt the improvement.

> I had enough equipment power, but I was using the wrong ability for the enemy mechanic.

> I entered a dangerous zone too early, damaged my equipment and decided to return to safer content until my build was ready.

> I specialized in a weapon because I enjoyed its playstyle, but I later developed another weapon to solve a specific challenge.

> I finally completed the zone after several cycles of gathering, crafting, mastery progression and build improvement.

These stories are more important than isolated numerical growth.

---

# 19. DIFFERENTIATION

Albion Idle should distinguish itself through the combination of:

* one persistent classless hero;
* use-based masteries;
* equipment-driven immediate power;
* integrated gathering and crafting;
* automatic but strategically directed combat;
* meaningful offline planning;
* readable power checks;
* zone-based chapter progression;
* escalating durability risk;
* frequent micro rewards within slow macro progression.

No single element is necessarily unique by itself.

The intended identity comes from how these elements reinforce each other.

---

# 20. PLAYER EXPERIENCE BOUNDARIES

The game must not become:

## 20.1 A passive waiting simulator

The player must regularly make meaningful decisions.

Time alone must not solve every problem.

## 20.2 A spreadsheet without adventure

Efficiency calculations may exist, but they must support visible character and world progression.

## 20.3 A crafting system with decorative combat

Combat must determine access to important progression.

## 20.4 A combat game with decorative crafting

Production must materially affect the hero’s capability.

## 20.5 A hidden-information puzzle

Strategic adaptation should be rewarded, but the player must receive enough feedback to reason about failure.

## 20.6 A permanent-class trap

The player must remain able to change direction and develop other equipment identities.

## 20.7 A multiplayer game without players

The project must not attempt to imitate player-driven systems through unnecessary artificial complexity.

## 20.8 An uncontrolled content clone

The project must develop an original identity rather than reproducing Albion Online content without transformation.

---

# 21. TARGET AUDIENCE

The primary target audience includes players who enjoy:

* idle progression;
* RPG character growth;
* crafting and resource loops;
* equipment optimization;
* build experimentation;
* long-term account progression;
* visible efficiency improvements;
* overcoming progression walls;
* returning to accumulated results.

The game should be approachable for players who do not want:

* continuous action-game input;
* mandatory multiplayer;
* competitive PvP;
* large raid schedules;
* complex MMO social obligations.

The game may still appeal to experienced RPG and MMO players through its optimization depth.

---

# 22. ACCESSIBILITY OF UNDERSTANDING

The game should communicate important information clearly.

The player should not require an external wiki to understand basic progression.

The interface should eventually explain:

* item effects;
* mastery progress;
* ability behavior;
* resource origins;
* crafting requirements;
* enemy threats;
* durability consequences;
* offline results;
* reasons for failure.

Advanced optimization may reward experimentation and community knowledge.

Fundamental rules should remain understandable inside the game.

---

# 23. TONAL DIRECTION

The intended tone is:

* adventurous;
* grounded;
* readable;
* progressively dangerous;
* satisfying rather than frantic;
* strategic without being academic.

The hero’s progression should feel earned.

The world should feel dangerous enough to make preparation meaningful.

The game should avoid becoming excessively comedic unless a later art-direction decision validates that tone.

The final narrative tone, visual identity and world lore remain open.

---

# 24. VERTICAL-SLICE EXPERIENCE GOAL

The first vertical slice must prove the following player experience:

> I encounter a challenge, identify an improvement, gather and craft that improvement, equip it and overcome the challenge.

The vertical slice does not need to prove:

* the complete five-zone progression;
* the final mastery tree;
* the full equipment catalogue;
* the final island system;
* the full worker system;
* final long-term balancing;
* final endgame;
* final art quality.

It must prove that the central relationship between adventure, production and preparation is enjoyable.

---

# 25. VISION SUCCESS CRITERIA

The game vision is being achieved when players can truthfully say:

* My equipment choices matter.
* My masteries reflect what I practice.
* Gathering and crafting help me solve real progression problems.
* I understand why I failed.
* Changing strategy can be more valuable than simply waiting.
* Offline time advances the plan I selected.
* I regularly unlock or improve something.
* Reaching a new zone feels earned.
* My hero remains the center of every system.
* I want to prepare for the next challenge.

---

# 26. VISION FAILURE SIGNALS

The game is drifting away from its vision if testing shows that:

* waiting is almost always the optimal solution;
* one build defeats every encounter;
* crafting can be ignored;
* combat can be ignored;
* masteries feel disconnected from player actions;
* equipment upgrades are not noticeable;
* players cannot explain their defeats;
* offline progression produces confusing results;
* zones differ only through larger numbers;
* workers or buildings overshadow the hero;
* the economy becomes the final objective;
* content must be hardcoded to function;
* the player has no meaningful reason to change strategy.

These signals should trigger design review.

# 28. GAME VISION SUMMARY

Albion Idle is a single-player idle RPG about preparing one persistent hero to conquer increasingly dangerous regions.

The hero improves through practiced masteries and crafted equipment.

The player gathers, refines and crafts because adventure creates concrete needs.

Combat is automatic at its foundation but strongly influenced by preparation, build choices and limited intervention.

Major challenges function as understandable power checks.

Failure teaches the player what should improve.

Offline progression executes a plan selected by the player.

The game rewards frequent small achievements while preserving slow, meaningful long-term progression.

The player should not feel that they won because enough time passed.

The player should feel that they won because they became ready.
